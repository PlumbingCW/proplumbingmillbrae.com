Toilet Float Repair in Millbrae, CA: How to Fix a Running Tank

A toilet that hisses, refills on its own or never quite shuts off often has a float problem. The float tells the fill valve when to stop, and when it sticks, sags or sits too high, water keeps moving.

This guide shows how to tell the float types apart, adjust the water level, and decide when a repair is a job for a plumber.

Lift the lid of your toilet tank and you will see a small group of parts doing a simple job. When the tank empties after a flush, a float drops, the fill valve opens, and water comes in. When the tank is full again, the float rises and the valve closes.

If the float misjudges that moment, you get a toilet that runs, refills by itself or hisses all night. Many of these problems come down to a float that needs an adjustment, a replacement or just a rinse.

Which type of float do you have?

The float comes in a few designs, and the fix depends on which one is in your tank. Look at the part next to the tall fill valve on one side of the tank.

Ball float on an arm

The old style, with a plastic or metal ball at the end of a rod. The rod bends or an adjusting screw changes the water level. It is simple, and it fails when the ball takes on water or the rod corrodes.

Cup float on the fill valve

A plastic cup slides up and down the fill valve shaft. A clip or screw sets its height. It is the most common design in homes today and the easiest to adjust.

Float arm with a pull rod

A thin arm moves a rod connected to the valve body. These show up on many fill valves of mixed age, and the rod can be bent by accident when someone moves the tank lid.

Pressure sensing valve

Some modern valves use no visible float at all. They sense tank water pressure instead, and the fix is replacement rather than adjustment.

Signs the float is the problem

A float fault usually shows up in one of a few ways. You may hear the fill valve hissing long after the flush, see water trickling down the overflow tube in the center of the tank, or find the tank water level so high it touches the top of that tube.

A low water level is the opposite problem. The bowl may flush weakly, because the tank is not filling enough. In both cases, the float is telling the valve the wrong thing, either because it is set badly or because something is binding.

Step by step: adjust the float

1

Shut off the water and look

Close the small valve on the wall or floor behind the toilet by turning it clockwise. Remove the lid and set it somewhere safe. Flush once to empty the tank and watch where the float sits.

2

Find the water level mark

Many fill valves and tanks show a line marking the correct water level, usually about an inch below the top of the overflow tube. If there is no mark, aim for a level just under the tube opening.

3

Adjust the float

On a cup float, squeeze the clip and slide the cup down to lower the level or up to raise it. On a ball float, turn the adjustment screw or bend the rod gently. Small changes go a long way.

4

Turn the water back on and test

Open the valve slowly, let the tank refill, and see where the water stops. Flush again and check that the fill valve shuts off cleanly. Repeat until the level sits just below the overflow tube.

5

Check for leftover problems

Listen for hissing and look at the overflow tube. If water still runs, the problem is not just the float. Time to look at the fill valve or the flapper.

Float, fill valve or flapper?

Many people replace the wrong part because the symptoms overlap. A quick test can separate them. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait without flushing, and look at the bowl. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper is leaking and the float is not your problem.

If the bowl stays clear but the water keeps running into the overflow tube, the fill valve or float is not shutting off. This table shows the usual cause for each symptom.

Replacing the fill valve and float

When adjusting does not help, the float and fill valve are usually replaced together as one assembly. The old valve unscrews from the bottom of the tank after the supply line is disconnected, and the new one goes in the same hole. It is a manageable job if you are patient with the nuts and the water line.

Set the height of the new valve before you tighten it so the top sits where the manufacturer says, and connect the supply line without overtightening plastic fittings. Turn the water on slowly and test the shutoff a couple of times. If the shutoff valve behind the toilet leaks or will not close fully, stop there, because that is a separate repair.

Millbrae water pressure and your float

Millbrae runs its own water system from San Francisco Regional Water System supply, and the city's system is divided into 11 pressure zones. That means water pressure at your house can differ from a neighbor's across town. High pressure pushes hard against a fill valve seat and can make a float valve fail to seal fully, while very low pressure refills the tank slowly and can look like a float fault.

If your toilet and a few other fixtures all show odd symptoms, such as faucet hammer or hissing, pressure may be part of the picture. We can test it at an outdoor hose connection. Millbrae's water is chloraminated, and rubber flappers and fill valve seals do wear out, so replacing them when they go soft is routine care.

The city's Public Works department runs the water service in Millbrae, and a running toilet adds to what is metered at your house. A small float fix is a simple way to protect your bill.

What you can do and when to stop

DIY or what people try

  • Bending a float arm until the water level looks right
  • Replacing the flapper when the float is the real fault
  • Tightening plastic fittings with a wrench until they crack
  • Leaving a running toilet for weeks and hoping it clears

Professional approach

  • Running a dye test to separate flapper leaks from float faults
  • Testing house pressure when several fixtures act up
  • Replacing the fill valve and supply line as a matched set
  • Checking the shutoff valve and bowl seal while we are there

Toilet float questions

Look for a tank that never stops filling, water running into the overflow tube, hissing after a flush, or a float that scrapes the tank. If a dye test shows color in the bowl, the flapper is the culprit instead.

On an old ball float arm, a gentle bend often changes the water level. Bend slowly and in small steps, because brass and plastic can snap. On a cup float, use the adjustment clip instead of bending anything.
