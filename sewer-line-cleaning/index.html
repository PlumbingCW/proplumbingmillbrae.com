Sewer backup: what is happening in the pipe

Every drain in the house joins one pipe that runs out to the city main. When that pipe is blocked, wastewater has nowhere to go and rises through the lowest opening, usually a basement or ground-floor tub, shower or floor drain. That is why a stoppage in the main shows up in several fixtures at once, not just one.

Kitchen sinks that fill when the washing machine drains, toilets that bubble when a shower runs, and gurgling from drains are the early signs. We handle these as urgent calls at night and on weekends too, and aim for a same-day visit when schedules allow.

Water in the lowest fixture

The tub or floor drain nearest the ground is the first to back up. If it happens as soon as water runs elsewhere, the main is the problem.

Several fixtures acting up together

A toilet gurgling when the shower drains, or a sink bubbling when the toilet flushes, means air and water are fighting for the same blocked pipe.

Slow drains after a cable job

If drains clear and slow again within weeks, the line may be narrowed by grease or roots that the cable did not remove.

Paper and wipes at the cleanout

If a cleanout is open and full, the blockage sits between it and the main. Wipes sold as flushable are a common cause because they catch on rough pipe and roots.

Sewer smell near the yard

A sour smell outside or a wet patch over the lateral can mean the pipe is backed up or broken. Keep the area clear and tell us when you call.

How we clear a main sewer line

We try to give you flow back quickly, but we also want to know why the line stopped. Clearing without looking is how the same backup returns a month later.

1

Stop the source and find access

We confirm which fixtures are affected, find the cleanout and set up to protect floors. If there is no cleanout, we find the best alternative entry.

2

Clear the blockage

A drum machine with the right cutter opens the line, matched to the pipe size and material. For a heavy grease or root problem, we may recommend hydro jetting to scour the walls.

3

Run the camera

We pass a camera through the cleared section to see the pipe wall. This shows roots, cracks, sags or scale that caused the stoppage.

4

Explain what we found

You see what the pipe looks like and learn whether it is likely to clog again. If there is damage, we explain the repair options and what each involves.

5

Flush and test

We run water through the system to confirm that the fixtures drain freely. Cleanup of the work area is part of the job.

Whose pipe is it in Millbrae?

Millbrae's code makes the owner responsible for the sewer lateral on their own lot. The city maintains only the short piece between the cleanout wye and the main, and only when the cleanout is in the designated location, either the sidewalk or within 2.5 feet of the main in a yard. If the blockage is on your side, you are the one who has to clear it.

The code goes further. An owner must clear an obstruction immediately on discovery or notice from the city, and install a temporary trap downstream while clearing or repairing. If a blockage cannot be cleared by the owner or a hired cleaner, it is to be reported to Public Works, and the owner reimburses the city for its costs.

Millbrae specifics when the main backs up

The city owns the sanitary sewer system and the Water Pollution Control Plant that treats its flow, and it shares a deepwater outfall with South San Francisco, San Bruno, Burlingame and the airport. A backup inside your house is not a plant problem. It is almost always a stoppage on the lateral or at the connection to the main.

Because the owner clears the lateral and the code sets a duty to act promptly, there is a practical reason to call a plumber the same day. Waiting can mean damage to floors and a city notice, and the city can bill costs it incurs on a blockage you could not clear.

A cleanout in a sidewalk or within 2.5 feet of the main in a yard is where the city's responsibility begins. If your cleanout is somewhere else, the lateral responsibility may extend further than you assumed. Ask Public Works, and we will note where yours is.
