Why hot water takes so long to arrive

The wait at a tap has nothing to do with how hot your water heater is. The water heater could be doing its job perfectly while the pipe between it and the faucet is full of cold water left over from the last use. You have to push that cold water out before the hot water can travel down the line.

The farther a fixture sits from the heater, the longer the wait. A bathroom at the far end of the house, a kitchen on another floor or a laundry sink in an addition can all be a long run of pipe away. A larger pipe diameter makes the wait longer still, because there is more cold water to clear.

The goal of an instant hot water setup is to shorten or remove that wait. There are three common ways to do it, and the right one depends on the layout of the house, the fuel you have and how many fixtures are affected.

Three ways to get hot water faster

Point-of-use heater under or near the sink

A small heater is installed right at the fixture, either as a compact tank under the sink or as a small electric tankless unit. It heats water on the spot, so the trip from the main water heater no longer matters. This is the quickest fix for one distant sink and the least disruptive install.

Recirculation system

A pump keeps hot water moving slowly through a loop of pipe so the supply line stays warm. When you open the tap, warm water is already waiting. A recirculation pump can be controlled by a timer, a temperature sensor or a push button so it does not run all day.

Tankless water heater placed closer to demand

A tankless unit heats water as it flows, with no stored tank. Placing it close to the fixtures it serves cuts the run of pipe, and because it does not store water, it can supply hot water as long as the demand fits its flow rating. Whole-house tankless is a bigger project. Our tankless water heater page lists what these systems involve.

No option is automatically best. A one-bathroom house with one distant sink rarely needs more than a point-of-use unit, while a long two-story layout can justify a loop. We help you match the fix to the problem instead of selling the largest system.

What an install actually involves

Every instant hot water setup has a few common pieces. Knowing them ahead of time makes the quote and the schedule less mysterious.

1

Walk the layout

We look at the distance from the water heater to the fixture, the pipe size, the pipe route and where a pump, outlet or unit could be placed. That tells us which option fits and where the pipe will be tapped.

2

Check power, gas and space

Electric units need the right circuit and breaker. Gas tankless units need adequate gas supply and a proper vent. Even small point-of-use heaters need an outlet, a cold supply and clearance.

3

Install, test and tune

The unit is mounted, connected to water and power, and tested for leaks and temperature. For a loop, the pump controls are set so it runs when you need it and rests when you do not.

If your plan is to change or move the main heater itself, our water heater installation crew can walk the whole layout with you. Bring up any remodeling plans early, because opening a wall once is cheaper than opening it twice.

What older homes bring to the job

Houses that were built decades ago were often wired and piped for different demands than the ones we have today. A bathroom that was once a single fixture room may now host a rain shower, and a kitchen that once needed one sink now has a dishwasher and a second tap. These changes are what make the wait obvious.

Older electrical panels can also limit your choices. An electric tankless unit can draw a large load while it runs, so a panel with little spare capacity may call for a different option, such as a small tank heater or a recirculation loop. This is one reason we look at the panel before we recommend anything.

Hot water you waste while waiting

Running a tap for a minute to get warm water sends useful water down the drain. A recirculation loop or a point-of-use heater cuts that waste, but a pump that runs around the clock uses energy, so timers and sensors matter.

Millbrae specifics for a new hot water install

Permits and who handles them

In Millbrae, the Building Division issues building, plumbing, electrical and mechanical permits, and its counter is at 621 Magnolia Avenue. The city says a permit is needed to install, repair or modify plumbing, electrical or mechanical systems, but its pages do not spell out every water heater case. Permits may apply, so ask the Building Division, or ask us what applies to your install.

Instant hot water work often touches more than one trade, since a new circuit, a gas connection or a vent can each trigger review. If a permit is needed, a California contractor licence matters. The CSLB confirms C-36 plumbing licences, and its small minor-work exemption does not cover work that needs a permit.

Millbrae water arrives from the San Francisco regional system and is treated with chloramine. Hardness in the latest city report averaged 62 parts per million, which is on the softer side. Even so, small electric elements can collect scale over time, so ask us about flushing and descaling when a point-of-use unit is installed.

Doing it yourself versus calling a plumber

DIY or what people try

  • Plugging in a plug-in point-of-use unit where an outlet already exists
  • Insulating exposed hot water pipes where you can reach them
  • Setting a timer on a pump that is already installed
  • Running the tap longer and living with the wait

Professional approach

  • Sizing the unit to the fixture and the incoming water temperature
  • Adding a dedicated circuit, breaker or gas line where it is needed
  • Venting and testing a gas tankless unit safely
  • Confirming permits and finishing the work to code

Electrical and gas connections are where do-it-yourself work gets risky. If a gas smell ever appears near a heater, leave the building and call 911 and your gas utility before anything else.
