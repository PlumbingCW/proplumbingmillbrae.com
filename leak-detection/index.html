When a hidden leak is an urgent problem

Some leaks can wait for a daytime appointment, and some cannot. Water that is still flowing, a ceiling that is bulging, or a meter that spins with every fixture closed all count as urgent, because the damage grows by the hour. We take those calls overnight and on weekends, and we aim to get a technician out the same day when schedules allow.

Slow leaks matter too, even if they are not an emergency right now. A faint damp smell, a patch of paint that keeps peeling, or a floor that feels warm in one spot can mean a line has been weeping for months. Finding it early is cheaper than repairing the framing, flooring and mold that follow.

A stain that grows after rain is not always the roof

A ceiling stain can come from the roof, an upstairs bath or a supply line. We check whether the stain changes when fixtures run, which separates a plumbing source from weather.

Sound of water with everything off

A hiss or trickle in a wall or under a floor usually means a pressurized supply line. Turn on nothing, listen at the stain, and tell us where you hear it loudest.

Warm spots on a floor

A warm patch can point to a hot water line leaking beneath a slab or subfloor. It is a clue worth reporting even if the floor feels dry.

Meter movement with everything closed

If the meter dial or low-flow indicator moves when no fixture is running, water is escaping somewhere. We then work out whether it is inside the house, in the yard or under the driveway.

Crawlspace dampness

Soft soil, a musty smell or condensation under the house can come from a drain or supply leak. The crawlspace often shows the problem before the rooms above do.

How we trace a leak without tearing things open

The point of detection is to cut as little as possible. We start with questions and simple checks, then use equipment only where it is needed.

1

Confirm it is a plumbing leak

We ask where and when you notice it and look at the stain, the nearest fixtures and the meter. This rules out roof, condensation and appliance causes before any equipment comes out.

2

Isolate the line

By closing valves and watching the meter or pressure, we narrow down whether the leak is on a supply line, a drain or the section between the street and the house.

3

Pinpoint with equipment

Acoustic listening, thermal imaging and pressure testing show where the water is escaping. We mark the spot so any opening is small and in the right place.

4

Open only where needed

If a repair is needed, we cut the smallest practical access and explain what the opening will look like afterward. Anything that needs patching, painting or flooring work is for a separate trade.

5

Repair or recommend

Where the fix is simple we can repair the line. If the leak is under a slab or points to failing pipe, we explain options such as slab leak repair or replacement so you can decide.

How Millbrae water service shapes a leak search

Millbrae runs its own water utility, buying all of its supply from the San Francisco regional system. The city's water system reports 11 pressure zones and about 69.7 miles of mains. Static pressure can therefore differ from one street to the next, and a house with higher pressure puts more stress on old joints and pushes more water through a small leak.

Water and sewer service in Millbrae is run by the city's Public Works Department. If the meter keeps moving with every fixture closed, we trace whether the loss is inside the house or in the buried line leading to it, and we tell you which agency or trade to involve next.

Millbrae specifics worth knowing

About two-thirds of the city's housing is single-family detached, and a large part of the stock dates from the 1950s building boom, when dwelling units rose from 2,783 to 4,803 in ten years. Homes from that era may still have original galvanized steel or early copper lines. Those pipes tend to fail at fittings and low points, and they sometimes leak where you cannot see.

Millbrae water arrives treated with chloramine and averages about 62 ppm hardness in the city's latest water quality report. That is on the soft side, so mineral scale is less likely to be what clogs or seals a pinhole. Leaks here are more often a matter of pipe age, soil movement and pressure than of heavy scale.

If a leak means cutting into walls or floors, permits may apply, and the Millbrae Building Division handles plumbing permits. We will tell you before we start whether the repair is likely to need one.
