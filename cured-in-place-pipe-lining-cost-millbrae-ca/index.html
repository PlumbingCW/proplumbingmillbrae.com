Cured-in-Place Pipe Lining Cost in Millbrae, CA: What Drives the Price

Cured-in-place pipe lining repairs a damaged sewer line from the inside, without digging a trench the whole length of the pipe. The price depends on the pipe and the site more than on the lining itself.

Here is what moves the cost up or down, and what Millbrae homeowners should know about the lateral.

Cured-in-place pipe lining, often shortened to CIPP, works like a new pipe inside the old one. A flexible liner soaked in resin is pulled or inverted into the damaged sewer line, then hardened in place. The result is a smooth, joint free pipe that seals cracks and keeps roots out.

People ask what it costs, and the honest answer is that we cannot name a number without seeing the pipe. What we can do is explain what drives the price, so you know what a fair quote includes.

What goes into the price

Length of pipe to line

The liner is sized and cut to the run you need. A short section under a driveway costs less in material and labor than a full lateral from the house to the main.

Pipe diameter

A four inch house line and a six inch line use different liners and different equipment. Larger diameters take more material and more crew time.

Access points

Lining needs a cleanout or an opening to feed the liner in. If your lateral has no usable cleanout, one may need to be installed first, and that is its own small project.

Condition before lining

The pipe has to be clean and clear. Heavy roots, scale or sagging sections need cleaning, cutting or hydro jetting before the liner goes in, and that prep adds time.

Bends, offsets and connections

Sharp bends, offset joints and side connections make the liner harder to place. Connections that tie into the lateral have to be reopened after the liner hardens.

Camera first, always

Nobody should quote a lining job without a camera inspection. A video look inside the pipe shows cracks, root intrusion, offsets, belly sections where water sits, and collapsed spots. It also lets you see the problem with your own eyes instead of taking someone's word.

The recording becomes your reference for every later decision. It supports a lining plan, shows whether lining is even possible, and gives you evidence if you need to talk with the city.

When lining works and when it does not

Lining suits pipes that are mostly intact. Cracks, leaking joints, small root entry points and rough pipe walls are good candidates. The liner is a snug fit and the repair reaches the whole run.

Lining does not fix a pipe that has collapsed, separated at a joint, or developed a major belly that holds water. A liner follows the shape it is placed in, so a sag stays a sag. In those cases, digging or a replacement method is the right answer.

Lining compared with digging

A trench repair opens the ground, removes the old pipe and sets new pipe. It is the more disruptive route, but it fixes everything, including bellies and collapses. Lining leaves your landscaping and driveway mostly intact, and it can be faster.

Neither is cheaper in every case. Cost depends on length, depth, what sits on top of the pipe and how hard it is to dig. A short, deep pipe under a patio may favor lining, while a shallow, collapsed pipe in an open yard may favor a trench. Our sewer line repair page explains both approaches.

What shapes the cost of a lining job

No two lateral lines are alike, so no honest quote starts before the camera. These factors do most of the work in a price.

Length and diameter

More pipe and bigger pipe mean more liner and more time.

Roots, scale and debris must be cleared before lining.

Access

A missing cleanout may need to be added first.

Connections and bends

Reopening side connections and handling offsets takes skilled labor.

Millbrae sewer laterals: who owns what

Millbrae's sewer system is owned by the city, and Municipal Code Chapter 8.20 spells out who looks after which part. The property owner builds, maintains and clears the lateral on their own lot. The city maintains only the stretch between the cleanout wye and the main, and only when the cleanout is in the designated location. That ownership line decides who pays when the pipe fails.

The code also says roots in a private lateral are the owner's problem. When the city requires root removal, the owner has 120 days after notice to remove roots and repair the pipe so they do not come back. Lining is one way to meet that kind of repair, since a smooth liner keeps roots out, though you should confirm the approach with Public Works.

Other sections of the code tie into lining work. A building permit that adds fixtures or enlarges the building by more than 25 percent can require a camera inspection of the lateral. A lateral sold with a house older than 20 years has also been subject to test and repair rules, so check current status with Public Works before relying on that. Permits may apply, and the Millbrae Building Division handles plumbing permits.

Questions to ask before you sign

Ask for the camera footage and a plain description of what the camera found. Ask which sections will be lined, how the liner will be cleaned and cured, and how connections will be reopened. Ask whether a cleanout will be added and where.

Ask what happens if the liner cannot be placed because the pipe is worse than expected. A good contractor explains the fallback before starting, not after. And ask what the city may need to see, since a lateral is a regulated part of the sewer system.
