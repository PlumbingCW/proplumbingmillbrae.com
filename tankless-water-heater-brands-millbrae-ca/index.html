Tankless Water Heater Brands in Millbrae, CA: What to Compare

Choosing between tankless water heater brands is easier once you know which differences are real and which are marketing. Most of what matters sits in the design of the unit, not in the name on the front.

This guide lists the features worth comparing, so you can judge any model your installer or a store shows you.

People often ask which tankless brand is best. We do not rank brands here, because the right choice depends far more on your house than on a logo. What we can do is show you the features that separate one model from another, so the conversation with any installer becomes concrete.

Well known makers such as Rinnai, Navien, Noritz and Rheem all sell on-demand units, and the names come up often in the Bay Area. Treat them as starting points for research, not as a verdict.

Start with the house, not the badge

Before comparing makers, three facts about your home decide which models are even possible. The first is the fuel: natural gas, propane or electric. The second is the route for venting and combustion air. The third is how much hot water you want at the same moment.

A unit that cannot be vented properly or fed by your gas line is the wrong unit, whoever made it. We check those basics first and only then compare models.

The features that actually differ

Condensing or non-condensing

A condensing gas unit squeezes extra heat from exhaust, runs cooler flue gas and usually vents through plastic pipe. It also produces acidic condensate that needs a drain and, often, a neutralizer. A non-condensing unit vents hot gas through metal and skips the condensate, but it uses a bit more gas.

Flow rate and temperature rise

Every model lists how many gallons per minute it can heat by a given number of degrees. Incoming water in the Bay Area is cool for much of the year, so check the flow figure at the temperature rise you actually need, not just the headline number.

Built-in recirculation

Waiting for hot water at a far bathroom is the most common complaint. Some models have a recirculation pump or a built-in buffer tank, and others rely on a separate pump. Ask what is included and what is an add-on.

Controls and connectivity

Remote panels, app control and error code displays vary. A clear code display helps a technician work faster. Fancy connectivity is nice, but a model you can diagnose easily matters more.

Parts and service access

Ask who stocks parts, how common the model is in your area, and how simple the flush ports are to reach. A unit that is easy to descale and easy to find parts for will cost less to keep running.

Gas, electric and what each asks of your home

Gas tankless heaters need a gas line large enough for the burner. Many older tank installs have a gas pipe sized for a smaller appliance, and upsizing the line is a real part of the job. Venting also needs correct clearances from windows, doors and neighboring structures.

Electric tankless heaters skip the vent and the gas, but they draw a large amount of electrical power at once, and many houses lack the panel capacity. In a home where the panel is already full, the gas version is often the more practical choice.

Questions to ask any installer

Ask which model they recommend and why it fits your pipe sizes, your vent route and your usage. A good answer mentions specifics about your house, not a general claim that a model is the best. If the answer sounds like a brochure, push for details.

Ask what the plan is for scale control. Ask whether isolation valves for flushing will be installed, because they turn a hard annual job into an easy one. Ask what manufacturer coverage and installation terms apply, and get them from the maker's documents instead of from memory. Also ask who will handle service later, because a unit with no local service support is a risk even if it is well built.

Millbrae water and what it means for a tankless unit

Millbrae gets its water from the San Francisco Regional Water System, and the city's 2025 water quality report shows hardness from 8.1 to 112 ppm with an average of 62 ppm. That is on the soft side, which means less scale than many parts of California, but not none. A tankless heat exchanger has narrow passages, and even mild scale reduces flow over time, so a yearly flush is still worth doing.

The same report shows chloramine in the supply. When we plan a tankless install we look at the fittings and valves on the water side, since they all touch treated water. Millbrae also runs its own water system with 11 pressure zones, so pressure can differ from one street to another. We check static pressure before sizing anything, because pressure that is too low limits flow and pressure that is too high stresses the unit.

On the regulatory side, Bay Area air district rules on new water heaters are changing, including rules aimed at on-demand gas units. Ask us what applies to your replacement before you commit to a model. Permits may apply, and the Millbrae Building Division handles plumbing permits.

What not to rely on when you compare

Be careful with comparison charts that rank brands by a single score. Many are built from the ads of sellers, and they rarely account for your venting, your gas line or your water. A model that looks best on paper can be the wrong answer in your utility closet.

Be equally careful about reviews of a single unit that you read online, because they describe someone else's house. What carries over is the pattern: how easy the unit was to service, how clearly it reported faults, and how well the installer handled venting.

DIY or professional

DIY or what people try

  • Buying a unit by name alone and expecting it to fit the old tank's gas line
  • Reusing old vent pipe that is not rated for the new unit
  • Skipping isolation valves, which makes later flushing a chore
  • Ignoring the condensate drain on a condensing model

Professional approach

  • Checking gas line size, vent route and clearances before choosing a model
  • Setting water pressure and recirculation to suit the house
  • Installing flush valves and a condensate path where needed
  • Strapping, testing and pulling a permit where one applies
