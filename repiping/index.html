Repiping in Millbrae, CA

Pipes wear out from the inside, and by the time you see rust-colored water or a pinhole leak, the rest of the line is usually close behind. We replace failing supply piping in whole houses and in sections.

Repiping means replacing the water supply lines that run through your walls, ceilings and crawl space with new pipe. It is not a drain or sewer job, and it is not something to do over one leak. It makes sense when the pipe itself is failing, so that patching one spot only sends the next failure somewhere else.

This page explains how to tell the difference, what the work involves day to day and what to ask before you hire anyone, including us.

Signs your supply pipes are near the end

Rust-colored or metallic water

Brown or orange water from the hot tap or both taps, especially after the water has been off for a while, can mean the inside of old steel pipe is corroding. A single sample does not prove it, but a repeated pattern does.

Low pressure that keeps getting worse

Galvanized steel can narrow from the inside as mineral and rust build up. The pipe is not broken, but less water gets through, and the showers and washing machine feel it first.

Repeated pinhole leaks

A pinhole in copper is rarely a one-off. If you have fixed one and a second shows up in a different spot, the pipe wall is thinning along its length and more are likely.

Brittle or cracked plastic pipe

Some CPVC lines become brittle with age and heat and can crack at fittings. If you have found a cracked fitting, the rest of the run deserves a look.

Water bill climbing with no visible leak

A hidden leak in a wall or under the floor can run for weeks. Leak detection is the right first step because it tells you whether a spot repair will do.

Repair a section or repipe the whole house

The honest answer is often "it depends on what we find." A single failed section of copper in an otherwise sound house is a repair. A house where galvanized pipe is corroded throughout, or where several leaks have appeared in a short time, is a repipe candidate.

We look at the pipe where we can see it, check pressure at several fixtures and test for leaks before we recommend anything. If a repair will hold, we say so and do the repair. If it will not, we explain what we saw so the choice is yours.

What a repipe involves

Repiping means opening walls or ceilings at strategic points, running new pipe, connecting every fixture and closing the access again. It sounds disruptive, and it can be, but good planning keeps the number of openings low and the time without water short.

1

Walk the house and plan the route

We look at every fixture, trace where the old pipe runs and decide which walls, attics or crawl spaces give the cleanest path. You see the plan before anything is cut.

2

Rough in the new lines

We run new supply pipe to each fixture, set shutoff valves and connect to the main. The water is off for part of the day, and we tell you in advance which hours.

3

Test, inspect and patch

We pressure-test the new lines, arrange inspection where a permit applies and patch the access openings. Paint and finish carpentry are usually separate trades, so we tell you what is left to finish.

Millbrae houses, pipes and what the city expects

Older housing and the ground it sits on

Housing data puts the typical Millbrae house at about 1958, and Census counts show that more than half of today's homes were already built by 1960. Homes that old have had a lot of time for pipes to wear, and some have been partly repiped already, which leaves mixed materials with joints where old meets new. The pipe material in any given house is a question for inspection, not for the year alone.

The San Andreas Fault lies within a mile of Millbrae's western city limits, according to the city's General Plan. Shaking moves pipes, so a repipe is a chance to use flexible piping and proper fittings that tolerate movement, and to strap and support runs properly. We explain the material choices before you choose one.

The city supplies water to Millbrae through 11 pressure zones, so pressure varies by location. A new repipe is a good moment to check pressure at the house and fit a regulator if the numbers call for one.

Repiping is permit work. Millbrae's Municipal Code requires permits for plumbing system work, and the Building Division handles plumbing permits at its counter and online. California's contractor licensing board, the CSLB, lists plumbing under classification C-36, and you can check any contractor's license on its website. The minor-work exemption is $1,000 and does not apply to work that requires a permit, so an unlicensed repipe is a risk to you as the owner.

Materials: what goes in the new walls

The common choices are copper and PEX, and each has trade-offs. Copper is rigid, long established and joined with soldered or pressed fittings. PEX is flexible, runs in long continuous lengths with fewer joints and is usually quicker to install in finished walls.

We do not push one material for every house. The right one depends on your walls, your water, your budget and what your building inspector accepts. Ask any plumber, including us, why they recommend what they recommend, and ask what the fittings are made of.

What people try first

  • Patching one pinhole after another with clamps or epoxy
  • Ignoring discolored water because it runs clear after a minute
  • Replacing only the visible section and leaving the old pipe in the wall
  • Hiring the lowest bidder without checking license or permit plans

What a proper repipe looks like

  • Test the pipe and the pressure before choosing repair or replacement
  • Replace the failing run end to end with a material chosen for your walls
  • Plan access, shutoffs and water-off time in advance
  • Pull the permit and schedule inspection before the walls are closed

Material choice

Wall and ceiling patching
